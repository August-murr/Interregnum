**Context:** This proposal was developed after a SPAR project mentor asked me to turn an older draft into a concrete research project. The original draft explored the falling cost curve of AI-enabled totalitarianism as a broad concern. This version narrows that idea to AI-enabled mass surveillance and translates it into a staged project plan in which each step can produce a complete, publishable result. It should therefore be read as an operational extension of the earlier idea rather than a complete treatment of AI-enabled totalitarianism.


## Situation

Advances in AI, and especially the falling cost of the compute behind AI, are going to make mass surveillance dramatically cheaper and easier to build over the next several years. 

The original motivation behind this project was the classic 1984-style prediction people make about AI, that it leads to full, Orwellian AI-enabled totalitarianism. But some of the other pieces a totalitarian outcome depends on are further away than the piece this project actually focuses on, which is why the scope narrowed to **AI-enabled mass surveillance** specifically. The full picture, one ruler or party locking in permanent control, backed by a fully automated, perfectly obedient military and police force, with all opposition crushed for good, depends on a huge concentration of political and economic power and armed forces that follow orders from AI or a small group without question. Most forecasts put that kind of transition years to decades away, and it's uncertain whether or how it happens at all.

Mass surveillance doesn't need any of that. It already exists, in every country, in some form, today. China's camera networks alone are estimated in the hundreds of millions. The US built a huge surveillance apparatus after 9/11 under a national security banner, and it's not just history: in 2013, Edward Snowden revealed the NSA had been running bulk collection of Americans' phone records and pulling data directly from major tech companies, inside a functioning democracy with courts and Congress. That's the clearest evidence this isn't only an authoritarian-country problem, it already happened once in a democracy and could happen again, especially as the tools get cheaper and more capable. None of this needed a totalitarian government, ordinary democracies and mid-tier states are already doing it, for reasons ranging from real crime-fighting to outright political control. What AI changes isn't whether this capability exists. It's how cheap, precise, and easy to scale it becomes.

Every government that ever tried to build a surveillance state hit the same wall: watching people takes people. East Germany's Stasi employed roughly one officer or informant for every 63 citizens, and it drowned in paperwork. The Soviet KGB ran a similar-sized operation across a much bigger population and hit the same wall. The bottleneck has always been making sense of all the collected data faster than it piled up, a job that used to require a human analyst's judgment and nothing else.

AI changes that specific thing. For the first time ever, AI systems are able to digest massive amounts of data collected from people, tens of hours of video and audio per person per day, plus personal information pulled straight from their devices, and actually analyze and flag that data, not just store and forward it. And the cost of doing this keeps dropping very quickly: GPU costs have fallen steadily for two decades, storage costs by roughly 90% in the last fifteen years.
	           
Today's costs still aren't low enough for most governments to run this at the maximum level the term implies, constant, full-population, every-device surveillance. The real question this project is built around isn't whether this becomes feasible, but when: when does the cost curve cross the point where it's practical for a given government, what will make us aware it's happening or about to happen, and what can actually be done about it, either to prevent or detect it in time, or to find ways to get the real security benefits this technology offers without opening the door to abuse.

## Strategy

This project has two sides: a **non-technical** side that explains why different governments want these systems, and a **technical side** that measures what it actually takes to build one and looks for ways to keep the benefits while cutting the risk of abuse.

The non-technical side starts with research into the history and motives of the different players, why a given government would want a system like this, what specific goal they're aiming for, and what they've already built so far. Motives turn out to vary a lot: some governments frame it as pure crime-fighting, some as counter-terrorism, and some, like China, build it as a routine tool of political control from the outset. That range matters, because it shapes what kind of intervention is even possible later. A government that's already treating surveillance as core statecraft isn't going to adopt a privacy-preserving standard just because a better one exists, but a government that's building surveillance for ordinary security reasons, without wanting to control its own population, might. Understanding who's who is what tells us where the later parts of this project can actually make a difference, and where they realistically can't.

The technical side is where the real measurement happens. The plan is to build a working prototype of a total surveillance system, using existing infrastructure and tools, fed with synthetic data standing in for the different kinds of information that could realistically be collected from citizens, video, audio, and personal data pulled from devices. That prototype isn't the end goal in itself; it's the instrument used to actually measure the cost of running surveillance at different levels of invasiveness, from the cheapest tier (public data like CCTV and traffic cameras) up to the most extreme (constant monitoring of someone's entire personal life). Those measurements then feed a forecast, a chart showing how the cost drops over the next several years, and at what point it becomes affordable for different kinds of governments, based on their budgets and technical capacity. All of the code behind this, the prototype itself and the model used to produce the cost forecast, will be published with the writeup.  Alongside that, a look at the infrastructure a system like this actually requires, data centers, communication networks, the rest, turns into a practical checklist: real signs to look for that would suggest a program like this is already being built or run somewhere.

Beyond forecasting and detection, the project should also look at solutions, and this is the part I think matters most. It would be lazy to treat the goal as just stopping these systems from ever being built. They're not purely harmful; done right, they could make people safer. The real problem isn't the existence of the capability, it's the risk of abuse and the loss of privacy that comes with how it's normally built. My instinct is that the right way to address that is technical, not institutional. Laws, courts, and oversight boards are supposed to be the check on this kind of power, but history keeps showing they bend or fail under pressure, secret courts approving surveillance programs that a real appeals court later called unlawful, export licenses meant to restrict spyware to "terrorism only" being ignored the moment the tool changes hands. An institutional fix depends on the people running it choosing to follow it, every time, indefinitely. A technical fix doesn't have to rely on that, if a surveillance system can be built so that raw personal data is physically incapable of reaching a human, and that guarantee is provable rather than promised, the protection holds regardless of who's in charge or whether the political will to enforce a law is still there ten years later.

## Project

The most important design decision here isn't what to research, it's how to scope it so there's always something finished. Rather than plan one big project that only pays off if everything goes right, this is structured as a series of levels, each one a minimal but complete and publishable piece of work on its own, that can be deepened or repeated if time and resources allow. If nothing else gets done past the first pass, there's still a real, finished result.

The first level is picking a player and understanding them. Rather than trying to cover every government at once, the project picks one to focus on for a given pass, researches their specific motives and history, what they've built so far, and what they're actually trying to achieve. The U.S. and China are the natural starting points, since they're the most consequential and the best documented, but the same structure works for the EU, Russia, or a middle power later. What comes out of this step isn't just background, it directly decides what the prototype in the next step should represent: a system built to match China's stated goal of "stability maintenance" looks different, in what data it prioritizes and how it's used, from a system built around the kind of national-security justification the U.S. has historically used.

The second level is building the prototype itself, at a specific level of invasiveness chosen based on the player from step one. The approach here is to actually think like the contractor who'd be hired to build this system for that government: generate synthetic data that matches the chosen invasiveness level, whether that's public camera footage or something closer to full personal-device monitoring, and then build a working system using real, existing tools, whatever mix of hardware, commercial API providers, open-source models, or self-hosted infrastructure a real contractor would actually use. The output of this step is a real, measured cost per citizen for running surveillance at that specific level, not an estimate pulled from someone else's report.

The third level is forecasting. Using the same tools and infrastructure measured in the prototype, combined with the existing, well-documented trends in how those specific costs (compute, storage, data transfer) have dropped historically, the project produces a forecast of how the cost of that same system changes over the next five to ten years.

The fourth level is infrastructure analysis. This step takes the prototype and imagines scaling it up to the size of an entire national population: what that would require in data centers, how much compute those data centers would need, what kind of communication networks would be needed to move that volume of data, and what collection methods would actually be used at that scale. This doesn't require building anything at that size, just modeling it based on real numbers from the smaller prototype.

All four of those levels feed directly into the fifth: detection. The motives, the real cost numbers, and the infrastructure requirements all come together into a concrete, practical set of signs to look for, things that would suggest a system like this is being built or already running somewhere in the real world.

A sixth level, further out, is solutions: technical approaches that preserve privacy or increase security rather than just documenting the risk. I'm genuinely skeptical this fits inside the time and resources available for this project, so it's treated as a stretch goal rather than a commitment, something to pursue only if the earlier levels leave room.

Once one full pass through these levels is complete for a given player, invasiveness level, and toolset, the same structure repeats if there's still time or resources left, this time with a different player, a different invasiveness level, different synthetic data, and possibly different tools. Each additional pass doesn't start from scratch: it adds detail and accuracy to the overall picture, and gives a real chance to go back and catch mistakes or bad assumptions from the previous round before they carry forward.
