# The AI Cash-Out

### Why slowing the frontier may require a transition agreement

*Written in collaboration with Sol.*

> **Working note:** This is a speculative argument. The quotations, estimates, timelines, and proposed compute ratios should be verified before publication.

> “I'm constantly asked why this isn't being done in isolation and by government!” — Dario Amodei

The people who founded frontier AI labs knew that governments were unlikely to lead an AGI project themselves. Governments were not prepared to take the possibility seriously, provide the necessary resources, or make AI safety a central priority. Private labs became the alternative: develop the technology, prove that AGI is possible, and force governments to respond once the implications become difficult to ignore.

This creates a problem. If frontier labs succeed, the transition may be too large to end through an ordinary acquisition or IPO. The eventual outcome may look less like a conventional startup exit and more like a negotiated transfer of power, ownership, and responsibility.

## Bailout or Cash-Out?

Some people suspect that AI is a bubble. If capabilities stop improving, that may prove true, although I consider it unlikely.

Even if an AI bubble exists, the dot-com bubble is a better analogy than the housing bubble. The collapse of the housing bubble damaged much of the global economy without leaving behind a corresponding technological foundation. The dot-com crash instead acted partly as a filtering process: many overvalued internet companies disappeared, while some foundational companies and technologies survived and continued to grow.

One prediction is that if an AI bubble bursts, AI companies will appeal to national security and ask the US government to bail them out. This draft is concerned with a different possibility. It is not exactly a bailout. It is a **cash-out**.

The cash-out would occur if frontier labs build systems capable of automating most jobs and severely disrupting the economy. At that point, ordinary markets and corporate structures may no longer be adequate. Governments may respond through major reforms, including centralizing control or nationalizing the frontier labs.

The shareholders could then argue that they should be compensated for financing unusually risky projects that the government was unwilling to undertake. They funded ideas that once appeared unrealistic, absorbed the risk, and built the systems that forced society to confront the transition.

This possibility may help explain part of the race to the bottom. If only the company that reaches transformative AI first is protected or compensated, then the race becomes winner-takes-all. A lab that slows down may not merely lose market share; it may lose its place in the political and economic settlement that follows.

## “Why Don't They Slow Down If They're in Charge?”

After Dario Amodei called for the frontier to be paced, many people asked a reasonable question: frontier AI companies control their own development, so why do they need the US government to make them slow down?

The answer may require reading between the lines. Lab leaders may not simply be asking the government to restrain them. They may be asking it to understand the situation and propose a credible transition.

One interpretation is that the founders, funders, donors, and early investors who understood the stakes never saw frontier AI as an ordinary venture-funded product company. They knew the US government would not begin a serious AGI project when the idea was still speculative. Their alternative was to build privately, demonstrate progress, approach AGI or superintelligence, and seek a settlement once the government understood what had been created.

Many observers treated these labs like normal startups: raise capital, develop a product, earn revenue, and eventually cash out through an acquisition or IPO. But if AGI produces the disruption its developers predict, the market into which the company would ordinarily exit may no longer exist in the same form. There may be no normal product business and no normal IPO. The economic system itself may have to change.

Lab leaders cannot easily state this directly. Publicly asking the government to prepare to acquire, protect, or compensate their companies would be politically explosive. Calls for regulation and pacing may therefore contain a second message: the government must develop a plan for what happens to the labs if they succeed.

## Toward a Real Plan A

Proposals to slow frontier AI are often framed mainly as a geopolitical problem between the United States and China. But there is a third important party: the frontier labs themselves.

No serious slowdown policy is likely to survive unless the labs receive credible guarantees about what happens to them during a transition. These could include protection against arbitrary nationalization, assurance that early risk-takers will receive some benefit, and a system that prevents the entire outcome from going to whichever company wins the race by a few months.

Without such guarantees, every lab has an incentive to continue racing. Even leaders who believe that slowing down is necessary may conclude that unilateral restraint would sacrifice their company without making the overall race safer.

The conflict between the US government and the frontier labs must therefore be addressed before an internal AI slowdown becomes realistic. Regulation cannot focus only on restricting what the labs may do. It must also define what the labs can expect if their technology becomes strategically or economically transformative.

## Oversight Versus Nationalization

Government oversight would leave frontier labs privately owned while imposing controls related to risks such as biosecurity, concentration of power, AI-enabled coups, and catastrophic accidents. It could constrain development and deployment without directly addressing the larger economic transition.

Nationalization would go much further. The federal government would acquire and control one or more frontier labs. Acquiring only one could create a state-owned competitor with an overwhelming and unfair advantage, so a serious nationalization plan might need to cover several labs. This could produce a total cost in the trillions of dollars, although any specific estimate would require much more work.

Such a payout would be politically difficult. The public could see it as the largest transfer of public money to wealthy technology investors in history. At the same time, governments were repeatedly asked to treat advanced AI as a state-scale project and chose not to do so. If private actors took the risk instead, compensation would become part of the argument over ownership.

Nationalization could resolve the internal race among US frontier labs by bringing them under one authority. It would not resolve the international race. That would require a separate structure—perhaps something closer to a **CERN for AI**, built around shared international development and oversight.

The central question for the federal government would be:

> Will technology developed by frontier labs create national-security threats or economic disruption that existing institutions cannot manage?

The government may not be ready to answer that question today. One alternative is conditional legislation: prepare acquisition powers, transition mechanisms, or other emergency measures in advance, but activate them only when a trusted third party determines that frontier systems have crossed specified capability or risk thresholds.

This would create a transition plan before a crisis rather than forcing the government to improvise after one begins.

## Internal First, International Second

The frontier race has at least three major sides, not two:

1. the US government;
2. the Chinese government;
3. the frontier AI labs.

The international race matters, but the internal relationship between the US government and its own frontier labs may be the more immediate prerequisite for slowing development. Before the United States can negotiate an international arrangement, it needs a domestic agreement about ownership, compensation, authority, and the conditions under which development would slow.

## “Solving Alignment” as Resource Allocation

“Solving alignment” can also be reframed more concretely. At least in part, it means allocating substantially more resources to alignment research relative to capabilities research.

The correct allocation remains unknown and should depend on the capabilities and risks of current frontier systems. When OpenAI created its Superalignment team, it committed 20% of the company’s compute secured to date over four years, yet the team reportedly struggled to obtain anything close to that amount before it disbanded in 2024. This suggests that the researchers involved considered an allocation on that order necessary. Twenty percent of total compute would equal an alignment-to-capabilities ratio of roughly 1:4, assuming the remaining 80% went toward capabilities. I would treat that as a reasonable minimum rather than the ideal allocation. As AI systems become more capable and dangerous, the capabilities-to-alignment ratio may need to fall much further, giving alignment research an equal or even larger share of resources.

Compute is not the only relevant resource. For problems that depend on human feedback, difficult judgments, or external verification, the important measure may be total resources: compute, researchers, experimental access, human evaluation, and time. I am more sympathetic to scalable superalignment approaches, which makes compute allocation especially important in this framing.

The technical question—“How do we solve alignment?”—can therefore be partially converted into a policy question:

> How do we give frontier labs both the incentives and the security needed to allocate far more of their resources to alignment rather than racing almost entirely on capabilities?

This is an unusual reversal. I often try to convert policy problems into technical problems that can be solved with scalable computation. Here, I am converting a technical problem into a problem of policy and incentives.

## Why Nvidia May Not Need to Worry

Jensen Huang has appeared concerned that calls to slow AI could reduce demand for Nvidia's products. But a slowdown in frontier capabilities would not necessarily mean a slowdown in data-center construction or compute demand.

If pacing the frontier requires labs to redirect more compute from capabilities toward alignment and safety research, the hardware may still be built and used. The objective changes, not necessarily the amount of infrastructure.

An AI slowdown could therefore mean slowing the growth of dangerous capabilities while continuing—or even expanding—the construction of compute for alignment research. The conflict is not necessarily between building more compute and slowing AI. It may be about deciding what that compute is used to optimize.

## The Core Argument

Frontier labs may be unable to slow down safely until they know what happens to them if they succeed. If transformative AI leads to nationalization or a new economic system, the labs and their investors will seek protection and compensation for the risks they took. Without a transition agreement, each company has an incentive to race for the winner's position.

This means that pacing frontier AI may require more than safety regulation. It may require a negotiated settlement covering ownership, compensation, resource allocation, and the transfer of authority from private laboratories to public or international institutions.
